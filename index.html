Creator-curated framework

CALCism — Cosmic Anarcho-Libertarian Communalism

Creator/reference name: Sora Izumi Dao (空 泉 道). Motto: “No theory is sacred enough to override reality.” This is the project's own curated framework, not a claim that CALCism is an established academic school or a published book.

Philosophical foundations: materialism/physicalism and methodological naturalism; critical inquiry and evidence; Buddhism's attention to impermanence and compassion; Daoist non-forcing (wu wei); and Confucian responsibility and relational care. These are selective influences, not a claim that the traditions are identical or fully compatible.

Political and economic orientation: anarchist communism, communalism, worker self-management, common/social ownership, mutual aid, free association, direct democracy and confederation. The proposed transition is Platformism → Social Mutualism → Social Collectivism → a stateless councilist confederacy, with voluntary associations, communes, councils and federations replacing coercive state hierarchy.

Ethics and method: moral anti-realism/error-theory-informed analysis; material consequentialism and practical virtue; attention to causes, evidence, effects and flourishing rather than treating “good” and “evil” as objective properties. This is a working synthesis, not a settled consensus: competing ethical theories and objections belong in the catalogue too.

Wider horizon: ecological responsibility, ecocentrism, cosmopolitan solidarity, anti-authoritarianism, feminism, cultural-philosophical syncretism and globally informed cooperation. CALCism treats its own claims as revisable: no tradition, theorist or ideology overrides evidence or material reality.

Use the CALCism Recommended List in the subject selector for a curated reading pathway. This panel records the creator's supplied principles; it is not an independently verified external source.

How to use the CALCism pathway

The CALCism Recommended List is a selective route through foundational works, related disciplines, practical organising and critical counterarguments. It is not a claim that every listed author agrees with CALCism. Use the subject selector in the Catalogue tab and compare works against evidence, material conditions and their consequences.

Transition model: Platformism → Social Mutualism → Social Collectivism → a stateless councilist confederacy. Core orientation: voluntary association, common ownership, worker self-management, mutual aid, ecology, cosmopolitan solidarity and revisable evidence-led inquiry.

Expanded political theory pathway

Anarchism, Labour, Mutual Aid and Social Revolution

This pathway brings together major anarchist traditions, their historical contexts and their internal disagreements. The catalogue now includes a much broader range of works by Pierre-Joseph Proudhon, Mikhail Bakunin, Peter Kropotkin, Emma Goldman, Errico Malatesta, Alexander Berkman, Voltairine de Cleyre, Rudolf Rocker, Murray Bookchin, Colin Ward, Nestor Makhno, Voline, William Godwin and others. It also adds works by Eleanor Marx and related socialist writers.

Read across disagreements: anarchists have disagreed over mutualism, collectivism, communism, syndicalism, individual autonomy, organisation, revolution, reform, violence, markets, federation, ecology and the relationship between class struggle and other forms of domination. No single author represents the whole tradition.

1. Mutualism, federalism and social organisation

Start with Proudhon’s What Is Property?, The General Idea of the Revolution in the Nineteenth Century, The Federative Principle, Confessions of a Revolutionary and The System of Economic Contradictions. Compare his mutualist proposals and federalism with later anarchist communism; do not assume all anarchists share his economic conclusions.

2. Anti-statism, collectivism and revolution

Read Bakunin’s God and the State, Statism and Anarchy, The Knouto-Germanic Empire and the Social Revolution and Marxism, Freedom and the State. Pair theoretical arguments with historical accounts of the Paris Commune and nineteenth-century revolutionary movements.

3. Anarchist communism, mutual aid and decentralised economics

Kropotkin’s The Conquest of Bread, Mutual Aid, Fields, Factories and Workshops, Modern Science and Anarchy and Ethics: Origin and Development offer complementary perspectives on cooperation, production, decentralisation and social change.

4. Organising, labour and syndicalism

Compare Malatesta’s Anarchy and At the Café, Berkman’s The ABC of Anarchism, Rocker’s Anarcho-Syndicalism: Theory and Practice, Ward’s Anarchy in Action, and works on revolutionary organising. Consider the trade-offs between durable organisation, spontaneity, federation, workplace power and local autonomy.

5. Anarchist feminism, sexuality and individual autonomy

Emma Goldman’s Anarchism and Other Essays, Living My Life and Marriage and Love connect anarchism with sexual freedom and women’s emancipation. Read alongside Voltairine de Cleyre, Lucy Parsons, feminist socialist writers and contemporary feminist/queer scholarship. Max Stirner’s The Ego and Its Own and Stirner’s Critics belong to a distinct individualist-egoist tradition; compare his critique of fixed ideals with collective accounts of care, dependency and structural power.

6. Eleanor Marx and working-class socialism

Eleanor Marx’s work, including The Woman Question: A Socialist Point of View (with Edward Aveling) and Shelley’s Socialism, broadens the catalogue’s coverage of women’s political writing and labour history. She was a Marxist socialist rather than an anarchist; her inclusion supports comparison between anarchist and Marxist traditions, not a claim that they are identical. Bibliographic descriptions of collected essays and pamphlets can vary by edition.

7. Social ecology and ecological anarchism

Bookchin’s Post-Scarcity Anarchism, The Ecology of Freedom and Social Anarchism or Lifestyle Anarchism connect ecological crisis with hierarchy, municipal politics and social transformation. Compare these arguments with Kropotkin’s decentralist ideas and contemporary ecological movements.

8. History, memoir and critical context

Goldman’s memoir, Kropotkin’s Memoirs of a Revolutionist, Bookchin’s history of Spanish anarchism, Makhno’s account of revolutionary Ukraine and Voline’s The Unknown Revolution offer historical perspectives. Memoirs and movement histories are valuable sources, but should be compared with independent historical scholarship and opposing accounts.

Catalogue years and titles can refer to original publication dates or later editions; check the specific edition. Some entries describe collections of essays, speeches or pamphlets rather than single-author monographs.

Expanded specialist reading pathway

Feminism, Queer Theory, Trans Studies and Liberation

This is a substantial, cross-disciplinary pathway—not a single school or a list of unquestionable authorities. It brings together feminist history, Black and intersectional feminism, decolonial and transnational feminism, socialist and materialist feminism, lesbian theory, queer theory, transgender studies, asexuality, abolitionist approaches and critical debate.

Editorial principle: feminist and queer thinkers disagree about gender, sex, identity, class, race, colonialism, institutions, capitalism, law, embodiment and political strategy. Read across those disagreements rather than treating “feminism” or “queer theory” as one unified doctrine. A work’s inclusion is not an endorsement of every claim it makes.

1. Feminist foundations and history

Start with Mary Wollstonecraft’s A Vindication of the Rights of Woman, Simone de Beauvoir’s The Second Sex, Kate Millett’s Sexual Politics, Betty Friedan’s The Feminine Mystique, and Gerda Lerner’s The Creation of Patriarchy. These works offer different accounts of education, socialisation, patriarchy, institutions and historical change.

2. Black, intersectional and decolonial feminism

Read bell hooks, Audre Lorde, Angela Y. Davis, Patricia Hill Collins, Kimberlé Crenshaw, Chandra Talpade Mohanty and Gloria Anzaldúa. This pathway centres how race, class, sexuality, colonialism and gendered power interact; it also challenges the assumption that one group’s experiences represent all women.

3. Socialist, materialist and care-centred feminism

Compare Silvia Federici, Cinzia Arruzza, Tithi Bhattacharya, Nancy Fraser and the Care Collective. Themes include unpaid and reproductive labour, capitalism, social reproduction, care, precarity, class politics and the possibility of a feminism that challenges economic hierarchy as well as gender oppression.

4. Lesbian theory, queer theory and sexual politics

Study Gayle Rubin, Monique Wittig, Michel Foucault, Judith Butler, Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick, Annamarie Jagose, Sara Ahmed, José Esteban Muñoz, Lee Edelman and Michael Warner. Their approaches are not interchangeable: compare social construction, discourse, performativity, normativity, desire, queer futurity and the politics of identity.

5. Trans studies, gender diversity and asexuality

Explore Susan Stryker, Leslie Feinberg, Julia Serano, Jack Halberstam, C. Riley Snorton, Judith Butler and Angela Chen. This section covers trans history, masculinity, racialised histories of gender, gendered stigma and asexuality. Trans and ace experiences should not be collapsed into one universal narrative.

6. Queer justice, abolition and political organising

Read Queer (In)Justice by Joey L. Mogul, Andrea J. Ritchie and Kay Whitlock, and Abolition. Feminism. Now. by Angela Y. Davis, Gina Dent, Erica R. Meiners and Beth E. Richie. These works connect gender and sexuality to policing, prisons, racial justice and abolitionist politics.

Comparative note: autonomy and collective liberation

Some feminist and queer theorists emphasise autonomy and resistance to imposed norms; others stress interdependence, care, material conditions and collective power. These tensions are explored across the catalogue rather than reduced to one shared position. The separate Anarchism & labour tab includes Max Stirner in the context of individualist egoism.

Comparative political and religious thought

Religious, Philosophical and Socialist Traditions

This pathway compares socialist, anarchist, egalitarian and social-ethical interpretations across religious and philosophical traditions, including Buddhism, Daoism, Confucianism, Hindu traditions, Islam, Judaism and Christianity. It does not assume these traditions are inherently socialist, anarchist or politically uniform. The aim is to study real historical movements, interpretive debates, anti-caste and labour struggles, communal ethics, and disagreements between religious, secular, socialist and anarchist thinkers.

Reading caution: “Buddhist socialism”, “Daoist anarchism” and “Confucian social ethics” refer to particular interpretations or points of comparison, not a consensus shared by all practitioners. Some works are direct political arguments; others provide historical, ethical or textual context. Read them critically and compare their claims with evidence and opposing scholarship.

Confirmed catalogue entry: Dhammic Socialism

Dhammic Socialism — Buddhadasa Bhikkhu is included under Religion, Socialism and Liberation Theology at Intermediate level (catalogue year recorded as 1993). It explores a Buddhist-inspired social philosophy; it should not be treated as synonymous with Marxism or as a position shared by all Buddhists. Check the specific edition and its licence before downloading or redistributing it.

Dedicated philosophy pathway

Moral Anti-Realism, Moral Nihilism & the Illusion of Good and Evil

This pathway examines whether moral judgements describe objective facts or express human attitudes, social practices and conceptual frameworks. Moral error theory holds that ordinary moral claims purport to state objective moral facts, but that such facts do not exist. Moral nihilism rejects morality in a specified sense, often objective or categorical moral truths. Moral anti-realism is the broader family of positions denying mind-independent moral facts.

Moral subjectivism grounds judgements in attitudes or perspectives; moral relativism makes them dependent on cultural or conceptual frameworks; moral naturalism is a contrasting realist family that seeks to ground moral facts in natural facts. These positions are not synonyms. Nietzsche critiques inherited moral systems but should not simply be classified as a modern error theorist. Sam Harris's The Moral Landscape argues against moral scepticism, so it is included as a contrasting position rather than evidence for anti-realism.

Known limitations

This is a static client-side site: saved titles remain in the current browser only; there is no account, cloud sync, database or admin panel. Search links do not guarantee that an edition is free, legal to download or licensed for redistribution. Live deployment must be tested separately after copying all three site files together.
